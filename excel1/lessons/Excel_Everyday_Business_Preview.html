Lesson 1: Organize Your Business Records

Find the information you need without searching through scattered lists.

Video slot: one guided demonstration, about 5–7 minutes. Recording script included in the package.

Start with a business question

Imagine finishing a busy day and being asked, “What did we sell to Bay Cafe?” The answer should come from one reliable list. In this course, you will work with Bayview Print & Gifts, a fictional small business. Its workbook contains sales, expenses, invoices, and a stock snapshot. You will use those records to decide what needs attention.

Open Excel_Everyday_Business_Workbook.xlsx and save a practice copy. Use the original to recover if you want to start again. The Overview sheet is the finished review view. Begin your work on Sales; the underlying records are what make a useful summary possible.

Give each row a clear meaning

On Sales, one row represents one sale line. The columns are SaleID, Date, InvoiceID, Customer, Item, Qty, UnitPrice, and Amount. Keep a single header row, one type of information per column, and a unique SaleID for each line. InvoiceID may repeat when an invoice contains several sale lines. The Invoices sheet will hold only one row per invoice.

Do not insert decorative titles, blank separator rows, or manually typed subtotals inside the records. Those belong outside the table. Keep the customer and amount on the same row. When sorting, use a control inside the complete table so every field moves with its record.

Make the data usable

A quantity such as 12 should be a number, not the word “twelve.” Enter the value, then choose a number or currency format. Formatting a text value as currency does not reliably convert it to a usable number. Dates should also be real Excel dates. The workbook displays them as month/day/year; use the date picker or an unambiguous date entry that matches your Excel language settings.

Use one standard spelling for each item and expense category. “Notebooks” and “Note books” may look equivalent to a person but create separate groups. The workbook includes dropdowns for items and expense categories. When adding your own categories later, update the dropdown list and any related summary labels together.

Use a Table for repeatable work

The workbook records are already Excel Tables. Click inside Sales and look for its table controls; the table name is Sales. For a new list, select the records and choose Insert → Table, confirming that your selection has headers. Menu wording varies slightly between Windows, Mac, and Excel for the web.

Open the Item header filter and choose Mugs. You should see three sale lines. Clear the filter to restore all 12. Filtering hides nonmatching rows; it does not remove them. Save your work with a clear name, such as My_Business_Practice.xlsx.

Interactive practice

Do it in Excel

  1. Save a separate practice copy of the workbook.
  2. On Sales, identify the header row (row 8) and the first record (row 9).
  3. Click inside the table and confirm its name is Sales.
  4. Filter Item to Mugs. Check that the three matching sale lines stay intact.
  5. Clear the filter and sort Date oldest to newest using the table header.
  6. Confirm that each SaleID still has its original customer, quantity, and price.
  7. Complete the browser repair activity, then save the workbook.
Check your result

The original Sales table has 12 sale lines. Filtering to Mugs shows three. Clearing the filter restores all 12.

Use it in your business

Choose one list you currently maintain—sales, jobs, appointments, or expenses. Write down what one row represents and the minimum columns you need.

Lesson 2: Calculate Sales Without Repeating Work

Let a reusable formula do the arithmetic when quantities or prices change.

Video slot: one guided demonstration, about 5–7 minutes. Recording script included in the package.

Replace repeated arithmetic with a formula

The first sale line contains 12 mugs at $18 each. Its amount is $216. On the unsorted original Sales sheet, Qty is in F9, UnitPrice is in G9, and Amount is in H9. Enter =F9*G9 in H9. The equals sign tells Excel to calculate; the asterisk multiplies.

If you sorted the table in Lesson 1, locate SaleID S001 instead of assuming it is still on row 9. You can also reopen a fresh practice copy for the row-number examples. Change S001’s quantity from 12 to 13 and its amount should change from $216 to $234. Restore 12 before moving on.

Let references follow the row

Copying =F9*G9 down one row produces =F10*G10. These are relative references: they follow the new row. For the second sale, the calculation should use that sale’s quantity and price. In a Table, Excel may show the same idea as =[@Qty]*[@UnitPrice]; the @ means the current row.

A dollar sign fixes a reference when it is copied. A shared discount rate might use $B$4, but the sale’s quantity and price should move with the row. Do not lock F9 and G9 if every sale has its own inputs. Check the first, middle, and last copied formulas instead of assuming the fill worked.

Total the right records

Use =SUM(H9:H11) to add three adjacent amount cells in the sample. The colon means “through,” so H9 and H11 are both included. For the whole named table, =SUM(Sales[Amount]) totals its Amount column. The original workbook contains $3,431 across August and September.

The Overview’s September sales figure is $3,021 because it applies date conditions. An ordinary SUM does not become a visible-rows-only total when you filter the table. Decide whether you need the whole table, a selected period, or a filtered subtotal before choosing the formula.

Check the result before relying on it

Test one line manually: 12 × $18 = $216. Then change an input and confirm the result updates. If you see a formula error, inspect the input types and references. Replacing an error with a typed number hides the cause and breaks the next update.

Sales in this workbook are billed amounts. A sale can be recorded before the customer pays. Payment tracking belongs on Invoices. The sample excludes tax and discounts so you can focus on the calculation; add those rules deliberately when adapting a real business template.

Interactive practice

Do it in Excel

  1. Use a fresh copy or find SaleID S001 after sorting.
  2. Inspect its Amount formula in the formula bar.
  3. Change Qty from 12 to 13 and verify Amount becomes $234.
  4. Restore Qty to 12 and verify Amount returns to $216.
  5. Inspect formulas in another row and the last row; each must refer to its own quantity and price.
  6. In an empty cell outside the table, enter =SUM(Sales[Amount]) and check $3,431; remove the temporary check afterward.
  7. Use the interactive formula lab, then save your practice copy.
Check your result

S001 is $216 at quantity 12, and $234 at quantity 13. With the original inputs restored, all sales total $3,431 and September sales total $3,021.

Lesson 3: See Where Your Money Goes

Review spending by category and period without adding receipts by hand.

Video slot: one guided demonstration, about 5–7 minutes. Recording script included in the package.

Keep expense entry simple

The Expenses table has one row per recorded expense. ExpenseID identifies the record; Date places it in a period; Supplier tells you who was paid; Category groups the type of spending; Amount holds the numeric value. Use the category dropdown so repeated records share the same label.

Start with the question, “How much marketing expense did we record in September through the review date?” Do not delete August to answer it. Keep the history and ask the summary to select the matching records.

Read SUMIFS as a business instruction

SUMIFS adds values that meet your conditions. The first argument is the amount column to add. Each following pair names a column to check and the value or condition it must match. For example, =SUMIFS(Expenses[Amount],Expenses[Category],"Marketing") adds all Marketing records in the table.

That formula returns $260 in the original sample because it includes both months. Adding the date conditions narrows the question. September’s two Marketing records are $90 and $95, so the intended result is $185. Use aligned columns from the same table so each amount is checked against its own category and date.

Keep the reporting period visible

Overview!B3 is the first day of the report month. Overview!B4 is the review date. The category summary includes dates at or after B3 and before the earlier of the next month or the day after B4. This includes the review date while preventing a later review date from pulling the following month into the report.

The workbook keeps those date controls fixed when formulas are filled down. In the formula for the next category, the category label changes while $B$3 and $B$4 stay put. The full formula is provided in the workbook; your job is to identify what it adds, what category it matches, and what period it includes.

Use the chart to ask a useful question

The Overview chart compares the recorded expense categories from a zero baseline. September totals are Rent $800, Supplies $485, Marketing $185, Shipping $110, and Software $60. Together they equal $1,640. Click the chart’s source range or inspect the matching category table to trace a bar back to a number.

A large bar tells you where spending is concentrated; it does not prove waste. Review the supporting records and business need before deciding what to change. The expense list and sales figure are practice operating records, not a complete profit calculation.

Interactive practice

Do it in Excel

  1. Open Expenses and filter Category to Marketing.
  2. Observe the August and September records, then filter Date to September.
  3. Add the two September amounts independently: $90 + $95.
  4. On Overview, confirm B3 is September 1, 2026 and B4 is September 25, 2026.
  5. Find Marketing in the category summary and inspect its SUMIFS formula.
  6. Confirm the five category totals add to the overall expense figure of $1,640.
  7. Clear any table filters and complete the expense explorer activity.
Check your result

September Marketing is $185, while all listed Marketing is $260. September expenses total $1,640.

Choose four or five consistent expense categories for your own business and one period you will review every week.

Lesson 4: Follow Up on Unpaid Invoices

Know who still owes money and keep the next follow-up visible.

Video slot: one guided demonstration, about 5–7 minutes. Recording script included in the package.

Separate the sale from the payment

The Invoices table has one row per invoice. Billed pulls the matching amount from Sales using InvoiceID. Received is the cumulative payment amount recorded by the review date. Balance is Billed minus Received. Recording another payment updates Received; it does not create another sale.

For INV1004, Billed is $450 and Received starts at $0. A $100 payment changes Received to $100 and Balance to $350. If an invoice already showed $120 received, an additional $100 payment would make the cumulative Received value $220.

Define overdue precisely

An invoice is overdue when a balance remains and its due date is before the review date. The training review date is September 25, 2026. INV1009 is due on September 25, so it is still Open that day. A zero balance means Paid, even when the original due date is in the past.

The basic logic is =IF(F9=0,"Paid",IF(C9<Overview!$B$4,"Overdue","Open")). This example assumes valid inputs. The supplied workbook adds checks for missing values, unmatched or repeated invoice IDs, and overpayments. Investigate a Check status before relying on the summary.

Make the next action explicit

Filter Status to Overdue, then compare balances and due dates. The original sample contains four overdue invoices totaling $960, including an August invoice. A monthly sales filter should not hide older unpaid balances from the invoice follow-up.

Use NextAction to record what you will do, such as “Confirm receipt and ask for payment date.” Put a real follow-up date in FollowUpDate. These fields move with the invoice row when you sort the table. A colored status is a cue; the action and date make the work repeatable.

Keep the snapshot honest

The workbook uses cumulative payment amounts and a stock snapshot. Changing the review date alone cannot recreate historical balances. For an earlier reporting date, you would need payment transactions and stock movements dated to that point. For this course, keep the date fixed unless an activity asks you to test the rule.

After recording the $100 payment for INV1004, outstanding balances fall from $1,812 to $1,712. Overdue balances fall from $960 to $860. The invoice remains Overdue because $350 is still unpaid. Restore the original $0 Received after this lesson so the final project begins from the same baseline.

Interactive practice

Do it in Excel

  1. On Invoices, locate INV1004 by its ID.
  2. Verify Billed $450, Received $0, and Balance $450.
  3. Change Received to $100 and confirm Balance $350 and Status Overdue.
  4. On Overview, verify outstanding $1,712 and overdue $860.
  5. Add a specific NextAction and FollowUpDate to INV1004.
  6. Find INV1009 and explain why its due-today status is Open.
  7. Restore INV1004 Received to $0 before the final project; keep or clear your practice action notes.
Check your result

After the additional $100 payment, INV1004 has a $350 balance. Total outstanding is $1,712 and overdue is $860.

Lesson 5: Monitor Stock Before You Run Out

Spot items that need attention before they interrupt an order or daily work.

Video slot: one guided demonstration, about 5–7 minutes. Recording script included in the package.

Choose a simple stock rule

The Stock sheet can represent products you sell or supplies you need to operate. SKU identifies the item. OnHand is the latest checked count. ReorderPoint is the level at which you review ordering. Target is the quantity you want after replenishment.

The rule for this exercise is review when OnHand is at or below ReorderPoint. Equality matters. Notebooks have five on hand and a reorder point of five, so they need review. The thresholds are teaching assumptions, not supplier recommendations. In your business, choose them using actual usage, delivery time, and storage needs.

Calculate the quantity transparently

For items needing review, OrderQty is Target minus OnHand. Mugs have four on hand, a reorder point of eight, and a target of 25. Their suggested order is 21. Notebooks need 25 and ink cartridges need six. Totes are above their reorder point and receive a suggested order quantity of zero.

The core calculation is =IF(C9<=D9,MAX(0,E9-C9),0). It assumes valid numeric counts. The supplied workbook also checks that inputs are present and the target exceeds the reorder point. A missing count is not the same thing as zero stock.

Use conditional formatting as a visual cue

The Action column displays Review reorder or OK. Conditional formatting highlights items whose status needs attention. Its appearance changes when the values change. Manually painting a cell orange would stay orange even after the issue was resolved.

Before placing an order, check the physical count, stock already on order, supplier pack sizes, and delivery expectations. A suggested order quantity supports the review; the workbook does not place an order. If your business sells services, use the same structure for office supplies, packaging, or other consumables.

Update the snapshot after a delivery

Receiving 21 mugs takes their on-hand count from four to 25. Change MUG OnHand to 25. OrderQty becomes zero, Action becomes OK, and the number of items needing reorder review falls from three to two.

This workbook uses a manual stock snapshot. Entering a sale does not automatically subtract inventory. That would require a stock movement system with consistent item keys and dated receipts, sales, and adjustments. Keep this starter process reliable by assigning a regular count update. Restore mugs to four after the lesson so the final project can repeat the delivery.

Interactive practice

Do it in Excel

  1. Find SKU MUG and check OnHand 4, ReorderPoint 8, and Target 25.
  2. Verify OrderQty 21 and Action Review reorder.
  3. Find NOTE and explain why equality with its reorder point triggers review.
  4. Temporarily change MUG OnHand to 8 and verify OrderQty 17.
  5. Change MUG OnHand to 25 after the simulated delivery and verify OrderQty 0 and Action OK.
  6. On Overview, verify only two items still need reorder review.
  7. Restore MUG OnHand to 4 before the final project.
Check your result

At the original counts, three items need review: mugs 21 units, notebooks 25, and ink six. After the mug delivery, two items need review.

Use it in your business

Choose one product or supply to track. Identify who checks its count, how often, and what information will inform its reorder point.

Lesson 6: Build Your Weekly Business Review

Turn updated records into a short, repeatable list of business actions.

Video slot: one guided demonstration, about 5–7 minutes. Recording script included in the package.

Start from a known baseline

Use a fresh workbook copy or restore the original inputs from the earlier lessons. Set Overview’s report month to September 1, 2026 and review date to September 25, 2026. Confirm the baseline: sales billed $3,021, expenses $1,640, outstanding invoices $1,812, overdue invoices $960, four overdue invoices, and three stock items needing review.

Investigate any Records needing correction before accepting the review. Also inspect required dates, amounts, and IDs in the source tables. That counter covers specific invoice and stock issues; it does not replace checking all source records.

Apply four ordinary business changes

  1. Paid sale: Add Sales row S013, date September 25, 2026, invoice INV1013, customer New customer, item Totes, quantity 5, and unit price $24. Amount should calculate to $120. Add INV1013 to Invoices once, with the same customer, due September 25, and Received $120. Billed should be $120 and Balance $0.
  2. Expense: Add E013 on September 25, supplier Parcel Service, category Shipping, amount $30.
  3. Payment: Change INV1004’s cumulative Received from $0 to $100.
  4. Delivery: Change MUG OnHand from 4 to 25 after receiving 21 mugs.

Add rows inside or immediately below each Excel Table and confirm the table expands. Inspect the new calculated cells. If a formula does not fill, copy the formula from the preceding row and verify its references. Never type a result over the formula just to make the total look right.

Check the new story

The updated results should be sales billed $3,141, expenses $1,670, outstanding invoices $1,712, overdue invoices $860, four overdue invoices, and two items needing reorder review. The fully paid new sale increases sales but leaves no outstanding balance. The payment reduces the existing balance without creating new sales.

Shipping rises from $110 to $140 in the September category summary and its chart bar updates. The new sale does not automatically change the stock snapshot. This separation lets you explain what each number measures and where it comes from.

Make the routine useful next week

Write three next actions with evidence. For example, follow up INV1004 because $350 remains overdue; review notebooks because five on hand is at the reorder point and the suggested quantity is 25; schedule a recurring review to update records, check exceptions, and assign follow-ups. Add an owner and a date so the actions can be carried out.

Save the updated workbook and download the activity’s review text. For reuse, keep a clean template, retain the table headers and formulas, and replace fictional inputs with authorized business records. Do not delete an entire calculated column. Start with one recurring task and compare the time and errors before and after adopting the routine.

  3. Add the $30 shipping expense.
  4. Record the $100 payment for INV1004 and the mug delivery.
  5. Verify each new table row, formula, summary total, and the Shipping chart value.
  6. Write three next actions with supporting figures, a person responsible, and a follow-up date.
  7. Save the workbook, download the activity review, and submit both if your instructor has enabled the assignment.
